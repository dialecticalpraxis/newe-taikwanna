## The Shoshoni Language

'Shoshoni' comes from the word, *[[sosoni]]*, which is the plural form of the word, *[[sonippeh]]*, which is a type of tall grass, found in the plains of Wyoming, from which many Eastern Shoshone built their houses.

### Number of Speakers

There is no consensus on how many Shoshoni speakers there still are today, but estimates from the 2010 US Census have the number around 2000 Shoshone who speak Shoshoni at home. The last monolingual Shoshoni as speakers died in the 1980s. Most of the native speakers of Shoshoni (i.e.,those who grew up with Shoshoni as the primary language in their home) also grew up speaking English and are all well over 60-years-old. There is almost no intergenerational transmission taking place, meaning that very few children are learning it, and even fewer, if any, are learning it to native-like fluency.

### Boarding and Residential Schools

Shoshoni, like many of the Indigenous languages of North America has suffered a devastating decline in speakers due to colonial policies of cultural genocide by the United States, Canada, and Mexico. The implementation of 'Indian' residential and boarding schools by the United States and Canada played a major role in the decline in speakers of Indigenous languages of North America. These policies violently kidnapped hundreds of thousands of Indigenous children from their families and communities, forcing them to live and study at a boarding school, many of which were run by Christian churches, and indoctrinated to assimilate to the dominant white Euro-American/Canadian culture.

Many Indigenous children died through this racist and genocidal policy that continued well into the 1970s. The generational effects of residential schools has been widespread; the trauma felt by even the youngest of children today. Indian Boarding Schools in the United States were active until the mid-1970s and many Indigenous cultural, spiritual, and religious practices were illegal until the American Religious Freedom Act of 1978.

### Oral Traditions and Written Documentation

The estimated more than 300 Indigenous languages of present-day North America all had one thing in common at the time of European expansion into the west. None of them were written. They all had oral traditions, including Shoshoni. This is not to say that cultures who do not develop a writing system are less than in any way; that would be a very Eurocentric way of looking at the world. Instead, it means that society and communication functions differently. Connection to each other and the natural world looks very different and the idea of knowledge and how it is obtained and passed from one generation to another is through the action of showing and doing.

The first written records of the Shoshoni language were by early Mormon settlers in what is now Utah. In 1859, Joseph Gebow, a European-settler who lived among the Eastern Shoshone for over 14 years, wrote a manuscript called, *Vocabulary of the Snake or Shoshone Dialect*. A second edition was printed in 1868. Throughout the rest of the 19th century and throughout the 20th century, contact with various Shoshone groups by missionaries, academics, and government entities produced written and audio-recorded materials, some that have been published into manuscripts, books, dictionaries, or used to obtain academic degrees, such as theses and dissertations.

Eurocentric ideas of knowledge gathering and ownership are completely foreign to how Shoshone view and experience the world, and thus the recording of language, which cannot be divorced from culture, spirituality, and knowledge, has many times caused more harm than good, and even though the intentions of white European settlers might have been from a place of good, the impact of this harm cannot be ignored. At the same time, there have also been those Shoshone who turned to recording their language by both writing and audio-recording it, seeing it as a way to adapt to some of the changes of learning by younger generations.

One such Shoshone person is Beverly Crum-naappeh, a member of the Duck valley Indian Reservation of Nevada and Idaho, located in Owyhee, Nevada. She was around 13-years-old when the late Anne (Nan) Smith, Alden Hayes, and Doug Osborne, three Euro-American researchers, arrived in Duck Valley on October 8, 1939. At the time, Nan was analyzing the mythology of the Great Basin through collecting and analyzing Shoshone traditional stories. Her 1940 doctoral dissertation was titled, *An Analysis of Basin Mythology*.

Beverly's parents, Tom-naappeh and Anna-naappeh Premo worked closely with Nan during her visit to Owyhee. Given how Nan describes the attitudes and demeanor of the Shoshone people towards her and her colleagues in her diary from 1939, they may have been some of the earliest Euro-Americans to come and write about the Western Shoshone. In fact, a folk etymology for the word *[[taipo]]*, meaning 'white person,' may be related to the word *[[tepooh]]*, meaning 'to write,' from the phrase, *atehi pe nemmiten tepoonna kimmaten*, 'those who come to write about us.'

The Shoshone stories collected by Nan Smith are published in a book called, *Shoshone Tales*. Nan hired and paid those who narrated the stories to her and the translators who helped to translate them. She did not record the stories in Shoshoni and so the book contains the translated English of Shoshone and Goshute stories, except for an afterward by Beverly Crum where she writes the story of *Coyote and Mouse* in Shoshoni and translates it.

Traditionally, the stories of the Shoshone were told in the winter and there are many who still believe that these stories should only be shared during the winter months, orally, and never translated. It is important to note that much meaning and nuance is lost when the stories are translated into English.

Beverly and her husband Earl-naappeh would go on to write down stories and songs in Shoshoni and translate them with both a one-to-one gloss and then an interpretation of the meaning from the original Shoshoni. Beverly received a bachelor's in 1975 and a master's in 1980 in linguistics from the University of Utah. She went on to work with various linguists and anthropologists, such as Wick R. Miller from the University of Utah and Jon Dayley from Boise State University. She and Dayley published *Western Shoshoni Grammar* and *Shoshoni Texts* in 1993, which described the grammar of Western Shoshoni and provided a anthology of different texts, including songs, traditional stories, and other descriptions in Shoshoni, described and translated in English.

### Revitalization Efforts

In the early 1990s, academic linguists sounded the alarm on the over 6,000 languages in the world, estimating that by the end of the 21st century, over half would be extinct. The past few decades have seen a concerted effort to preserve and revitalize the Shoshoni language. In 2004, professors Mauricio Mixco and Marianna Di Paolo at the University of Utah received a National Science Foundation (NFS) grant to preserve the original Wick R. Miller Collection recordings. It was called the Shoshoni Language Project. Wick Miller had started documenting and recording Shoshoni and Goshute in 1965 and created one of the largest collections of Shoshoni language materials before his untimely death in 1994.

Given that many of the materials were reel-to-reel and tapes, their shelf-life was limited. The NSF grant allowed Mixco and Di Paolo to digitize the recordings, transcribe and gloss the original Shoshoni, and provide translations for all the Shohoni text. The Shoshoni Language Project continued its work with preserving the unpublished field notes and recordings through funding by the Barrick Gold Corporation, now called Nevada Gold. During its time, the project developed high school-level Shoshoni language curricula, college-level curricula, and language materials for learning, such as a talking dictionary, story books based on traditional Shoshone stories, teacher-training workshops, among other resources.

Many of the resources developed at the Shoshoni Language Project were developed by Shoshone and Goshute high school students who participated in a six-week-long summer internship at the University of Utah where they took a two-hour Shoshoni course each day, worked closely with Shoshone and Goshute Elders on projects creating Shoshoni-learning resources, participated in social and cultural outings, and participated in professional development opportunities with departments from around the University. This program, created by Katherine Matsumoto who was a doctoral student at the time, was called the Shoshone-Goshute Youth Language Apprenticeship Program (SYLAP). SYLAP ran for seven years from 2009 until 2016.

While the Shoshoni Language Project was settler Euro-American-led, there have been other efforts led by Shoshone in an attempt to revitalize the language and culture. Samuel Broncho and Norman Cavannaugh (Duck Valley) launched the Shoshone Community Language Initiative in 2017, which was a five-week intensive Shoshoni langauge and culture program hosted at the Great Basin College in Elko, Nevada. The Shoshone-Bannock Tribes operate a public Shoshone-Bannock language immersion school called Chief Tahgee Elementary Academy on the Fort Hall Indian Reservation near Pocatello, Idaho.

### Linguistic Genealogy

Shoshoni is part of the Numic branch of languages, which is the northernmost branch of the Uto-Aztecan language family. In the sub-field of linguistics called, historical linguistics, languages are described as belonging to families much like humans are. Languages have a parent, siblings, cousins, etc. For example, English belongs to the Germanic branch of the Indo-European family of languages.

The Uto-Aztecan family is a language family, much like Indo-European is a language family, that spans from modern-day Idaho to Northern Costa Rica (see [[#^utoaztecanmap|Figure 1]]) and consists of over 30 distinct languages and includes languages like Shoshoni, Paiute, Ute, Hopi, Comanche, O'odham, Yaqui, Pipil, and Nahuatl.

The Numic branch of Uto-Aztecan languages is made up of three branches, including Central Numic, Southern Numic, and Western Numic. The Shoshonean languages make up a dialect chain that is partially mutually intelligible to speakers of the two other languages that belong to the Central Numic branch of Uto-Aztecan languages: Timbisha and Comanche. The word 'Numic' comes from the cognate word for 'person.' [[#^numiccognate|Table 1]] shows some examples of the word for 'person' in some of the Numic languages.

By understanding how languages are related or unrelated to each other, we can better understand migration patterns of people from thousands of years ago. Work by Dr. Katherine Fowler, published in 1972, points to the linguistic homeland of Proto-Numic as being somewhere near present-day Owens Valley, just east of the Sierra Nevada in present-day California. By reconstructing words from the present Numic languages into Proto-Numic, a combination of words for the ecology of this area matches with the time period she studied, approximately 1000-2000 years ago. This means that before the Numic languages started to break up into the individual languages that they are now, they were all one language, having descended from Proto-Uto-Aztecan.

## The Shoshone People

There are four cultural groups of Shoshone that can further be classified into traditional bands, based on the location of their traditional homelands and their primary food source.

### Eastern Bands

The Eastern Shoshone people were traditionally located in present-day Wyoming, having crossed the Rocky Mountains into the plains by the year 1500. By the end of the 17th century, a band of Eastern Shoshone were pushed into the southern plains of southeast Colorado, western Oklahoma and northwest and central Texas. This band is now known as the Comanche.

There were three major bands of Eastern Shoshone, the Buffalo-eaters (*[[kuittsutekka]]*), the Sage Grass People (*[[pohoko'i]]*), and the Sheep-eaters (*[[tukkutekka]]*), also called the Mountain People (*[[toyahinee]]*).The latter two bands joined the Northern bands of Shoshone.
