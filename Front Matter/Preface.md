# Preface

Fourteen years ago, I loaded up a truck and and drove out to Salt Lake City from Ohio. I was starting my master’s of linguistics at the University of Utah that August. Unbeknownst to me, I would spend four years in Utah, which would forever change my life.

This change came, not from my course work or research, but from the relationships that I made with people who shared their knowledge, culture, and language with me. While working as the Project Manager of the Shoshoni Language Project — during my studies — I met many Shoshone and Goshute folks who taught me more about life than all of my previous education combined. One of these people was Bryan Jay Hudson Jr.-[[naappeh]][^naappeh].

Bryan was a force. He lit up the room when he walked in. He had more charisma than a bonfire on a cold night. There was never a person he wouldn’t talk to, unless they had done him dirty in some way. Bryan was a kindred spirit. His love of languages went far beyond my own passions, and I was always inspired and in awe of him when it came to talking about languages, linguistics, and their place in the cultures of people from all over the world.

Bryan was one of the youngest fluent Shoshoni[^shoshoni] speakers, having learned it from hanging out with Shoshone Elders during the times he spent at his grandma’s. After graduating from the University of Oregon, Bryan went to teach English in Korea where he learned to speak Korean. He was able to break languages down to their very basic morphemes (smallest meaningful parts), which helped him to analyze and learn them deeper than your average language learner. His passion for sharing knowledge was contagious.

During my time at the Shoshoni Language Project, Bryan — among other things — taught a two-hour Shoshone course, five days a week, for six weeks, during the Shoshone/Goshute Youth Language Apprenticeship Program (SYLAP) that took place every summer. During SYLAP, we brought Shoshone and Goshute high school students to improve their knowledge of the Shoshoni language and culture, as well as work as apprentices on various language-learning projects, such as a talking dictionary and children’s story books that were based on traditional Shoshone stories.

The students loved Bryan. His love and passion for languages was second to his love for music. Bryan was a gifted and talented singer. Over the years of his life, he had learned round dance songs, not only in Shoshone, but in other languages too, like Ute, Paiute, and Cree. He loved to drum and sing round dance songs, always explaining what the songs meant and the significance of the words and how language was used. Singing is a way for knowledge to be passed from one person to another and to honor the relations that the Shoshone have with the natural world.

He was like a sponge that was filled with way too much water and nowhere to squeeze it out. His love for the Shoshone people, their language, and their culture fed his soul and sustained him. Bryan touched so many people’s lives and contributed so much toward the work that other Shoshone and non-Shoshone folks were doing for Shoshoni language revitalization, all while navigating systems of European settler-colonialism that tried to tear him down at every turn.

Bryan came to me back in 2012 with an idea to create a website called, *[[newe|Newe]]* *[[taikwanna|Taikwanna]]*, where we would post short lessons on how to speak the Shoshoni language. Our goal was to eventually publish a book that focused on the Western Shoshoni dialects. As with most things, life got in the way, and the project stalled after only getting as far as publishing a short lesson on reading and writing Shoshone. After Bryan's passing in late 2023, I had been trying to find a way to honor his memory, and what better way than finishing the project we started over a decade ago.

## Who This Book Is For

The Shoshoni language cannot be separated from a contemporary and historical context of violent policies of cultural genocide within a framework of continuing colonization and United States imperialism. The Shoshone people, ever so resilient, must constantly push back against an occupying government whose whole purpose is to see them all but extinct, their culture reduced to history books, their songs and language all but forgotten, collecting dust on a shelf.

When you make the decision to study and learn the Shoshoni language you are taking on a great honor and a great responsibility. Shoshoni is sacred. It is intimately linked to the *newenee*[^newenee]. It cannot be separated from their culture or from the lands and natural world from which it was developed. So be mindful when taking on the responsibility of learning one of the most fascinating and beautiful languages in the world, because it is more than just learning words, phrases, and linguistic structures. It is a gift and a privilege to be able to peak inside the very heart of who the Shoshone are and must be done with great respect and attention.

This book is intended for those who are ready to take on this responsibility. In our minds, it is someone who is *newe*, or someone who already has relationships with someone who is *newe* and would like to learn the language of their friends, partners, and/or family. Whether you don't know a single word in Shoshoni or are conversational, this book is for you. Whether you haven't necessarily been particularly good at learning a second language or whether you're a linguist or polyglot, this book is for you. We wish you well on this journey and ask that you go forward with a good mind.

## How to Use This Book

This book intends to provide a foundational introduction to the Western dialect of the Shoshoni language as it has been spoken for the past 60 or so years. It is organized in three parts. The first part is a general introduction to language and language families. It provides a more in depth introduction to the Shoshoni language, the Shoshone people, and the geography and ecology of the Shoshone homelands.

The second part provides lessons on speaking Shoshoni. It begins with introducing the sounds of Shoshoni and how to read and write Shoshoni, using the Beverly Crum-naappeh and the late Wick R. Miller writing system. For centuries, Shoshoni and many languages around the world have only been spoken, and Shoshoni maintains that oral tradition. Learning to read and write in Shoshoni should be seen as a tool towards improving one's speaking and listening.

The following lessons in part 2 will then provide you an opportunity to learn useful vocabulary, phrases, and grammar, while also providing information about cultural traditions and every-day life of a *newe*, living in the Great Basin. Each lesson will include dialogues, new vocabulary, grammatical concepts, cultural concepts, and practice.

The third part of this book provides reference materials to aid the learner in their journey. It includes a reference grammar of Shoshoni, a glossary of grammatical terms, a Shoshoni-English dictionary, as well as an English-Shoshoni dictionary, a bibliogrpahy of resources to further study,and finally an index to aid a learner in finding a specific topic.

We trust that you will use this book to the fullest and that you will go at a pace that is most comfortable to you. Everyone learns language differently. The more you expose yourself to it, the easier it will become.

[^naappeh]: -naappeh is used with names for people who have passed away. It means 'late' or 'deceased.'
[^shoshoni]: The Shoshone refer to themselves as *newe*, which means 'people,' and in English they are referred to as the Shoshone and sometimes Shoshoni. For the purposes of this book, 'Shoshoni' is used to refer to the language and 'Shoshone' is used to refer to the culture and people.
[^newenee]: plural of *newe*.
